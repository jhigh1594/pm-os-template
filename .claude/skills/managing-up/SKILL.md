# Managing Up

Help the user work effectively with their manager and executives using strategies from 35 product leaders.

## How to Help

When the user asks for help managing up:

1. **Understand the relationship** - Ask about their manager's style, what they care about, and where the friction is
2. **Diagnose the gap** - Is this a communication issue, alignment issue, trust issue, or visibility issue?
3. **Apply the right approach** - Proactive updates, reframing conversations, building trust through wins, or direct feedback
4. **Build sustainable habits** - Guide toward ongoing practices, not one-time fixes

## Core Principles

### Your manager is a resource, not an obstacle
Boz: "The advice I give more frequently than any other is for people to more directly leverage their leaders."

Your job is results. Your manager has authority to clear blockers. Ask them to bulldoze obstacles rather than trying to solve everything yourself.

**How to apply**: When stuck, ask explicitly: "I need you to clear this — can you unblock X by talking to Y?"

### Proactively communicate before they have to ask
Casey Winters: "People just way under communicate upward. Then they complain that executives are out of touch when they aren't telling executives what they need to know."

If your manager has to ask what you're working on, you're already behind. Weekly updates build the ambient trust that gives you autonomy when it matters.

**The HPM Format** (Boz / Facebook): A consistent weekly structure:
- **H**ighlight — the most important thing happening
- **P**eople — team dynamics, who needs attention
- **M**e — what you personally need help with

Add: "No response required" at the top. This signals you're informing, not requesting.

### Understand two levels up
Fareed Mosavat: "You should understand your boss's priorities and your boss's boss's priorities. Eventually, that means you have to know what the board is thinking."

Build a mental model of how your work creates leverage in the larger system. Tailor your communication to the concerns two levels above you.

**How to apply**: Frame your updates with the question: "How does this connect to what my skip-level cares about right now?"

### Bring recommendations, not just problems
Wes Kao: "When you ask 'Hey manager, what should we do?' you're putting a lot of cognitive load on them. Instead say 'Hey manager, here's what I think we should do.'"

Always arrive with a point of view, even a tentative one. Raw data without interpretation is noise. Provide insight and a recommendation, then invite pushback.

### Start with Chapter 1 when talking to executives
Casey Winters: "You have to start with chapter one — what part of the company strategy are you working on? What metrics are you trying to improve? What assumptions are you making?"

Find the last point that's obvious to the audience and build from there. Don't dive into tactical details ("Chapter 6") without establishing strategic context ("Chapter 1") first.

**Template for exec updates**:
1. Which company/product bet does this serve?
2. What metric are we moving?
3. What assumption are we operating under?
4. What we did / what we learned / what's next

### Position yourself as an ally who reduces burden
Ethan Evans: "Management can be a lonely job. Having an ally is a huge weight off people's shoulders."

Move from "How can I help?" to suggesting specific solutions. Proactively fix problems before they escalate to your manager. Leaders remember who makes their job lighter.

### Use concrete artifacts, not abstract ideas
Dylan Field: "The more concrete an artifact is or the more you can debate something, the better. I ask for examples a lot."

Bring docs, mocks, or data — not verbal descriptions of what you're thinking. If you don't have data for a follow-up question, pause and say so rather than guessing.

### Say what you'll do, say you're doing it, say you did it
Peter Deng: "Say you're going to do the thing, say that you're doing the thing, and then say that you did it."

This repetitive communication feels redundant but ensures alignment and creates checkpoints for course correction.

### Run experiments to challenge strong opinions
Itamar Gilad: "If you run a secret experiment and come back with data, either they get extremely mad at you... or more commonly, they're pleasantly surprised."

Evidence flips opinions better than debate. When leadership is anchored, design a small test that proves or disproves the assumption rather than arguing.

### Understand the spirit, then offer better options
Jiaona Zhang: "It's understanding the spirit of what they're trying to achieve. Being able to go back with 'I understand the spirit, but here's a better way to achieve it.'"

When you disagree with a directive, don't say no. Confirm the underlying goal first, then present a better path to that same goal.

### Build trust through the hot takes document
Ami Vora: "Once a quarter or so for the first year, I'd write a document that was just like, hey, for sake of provocation, if we wanted to fundamentally change a few things..."

Especially useful during onboarding or early in a new role. A structured "provocations" doc demonstrates strategic thinking and builds credibility faster than incremental wins.

## Diagnosing the Problem

| Symptom | Root Cause | Fix |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| Manager keeps asking "what are you working on?" | Under-communicating | Start HPM weekly updates |
| Decisions get reversed by leadership | Not starting with Chapter 1 | Lead with strategy, not tactics |
| Manager not clearing blockers | Not asking explicitly | Name the ask: "I need you to unblock X" |
| Disagreements feel like battles | Fighting opinions with opinions | Run an experiment; bring data |
| Manager surprised by bad news | Waiting to share until it's solved | Share early, with initial hypothesis |
| Limited access to skip-level | Invisible to two levels up | Ask manager to include you in relevant conversations |

## New Product Context

When metrics are still being established (pre-product-market-fit, new product area):

- **Lead with narrative, not numbers.** "Here's what we're learning" is a valid update when there are no metrics yet.
- **Make your bets explicit.** State assumptions clearly. This lets leadership engage on strategy, not second-guess execution.
- **Define success criteria together.** Don't wait for metrics to emerge — propose them. "I'm defining success for this quarter as X. Does that match your expectation?"
- **Use the hot takes approach early.** In a new product area, your fresh perspective is an asset. Document and share it.

## Questions to Help Users

- "What does your manager care most about right now? What's keeping them up at night?"
- "How does your manager prefer to receive information — email, Slack, meetings?"
- "Do they know what you're working on this week without having to ask?"
- "When you bring problems, are you also bringing a recommendation?"
- "What does success look like for your team from your manager's perspective?"
- "Have you asked your manager directly how they'd like you to communicate?"
- "What do two levels up care about that you could connect your work to?"

---

## Common Mistakes to Flag

- **Under-communicating** — If your manager has to ask what's going on, you're not communicating enough. Proactive updates build trust.
- **Bringing problems without recommendations** — Always come with a point of view, even a tentative one.
- **Starting with details, not strategy** — Start with Chapter 1 (strategy) before Chapter 6 (details).
- **Trying to solve everything alone** — Your manager has tools and authority you don't. Leverage them.
- **Waiting to be managed** — Managing up is a proactive skill. The most senior people got there by being great at it.
- **Fighting opinions with opinions** — Use experiments and data to shift perspective, not debate.
