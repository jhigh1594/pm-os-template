# Systems Thinking

Apply systems thinking to complex problems using frameworks and insights from product leaders.

## How to Help

When the user asks for help with systems thinking:

1. **Map the system** — Identify all players, their incentives, and how they interact
2. **Identify stocks and flows** — Understand what accumulates (stocks) and what moves between states (flows)
3. **Trace second-order effects** — Work through what happens after the first-order impact
4. **Find leverage points** — Identify where small interventions create large systemic changes

---

## Core Principles

### See the System
> "What does it mean to be a strategic thinker? It means to see the system." — Seth Godin

Understanding the invisible rules, culture, and interoperability that govern how products and organizations succeed or fail is the foundation of strategic thinking. Don't just look at the immediate problem—look at the system that created it.

### Think About All Players and Incentives
> "Systems thinking. Think of all the players in the system, think of all of their incentives and how they interact with each other." — Sriram Krishnan

This approach is superior to Jobs-to-be-Done for handling complex product trade-offs and multi-agent incentives. Every player responds to their own incentives, not yours.

**How to apply**: When analyzing a platform or ecosystem, list every player type (buyers, sellers, admins, developers, etc.) and explicitly state what each one wants.

### Use Stocks and Flows
> "Systems thinking is basically you try to think about stocks and flows. Stocks are things that accumulate and flows are the movement from a stock to another thing." — Will Larson

Model business processes like hiring pipelines or user funnels using this framework:
- **Stocks**: What accumulates? (users in onboarding, bugs in backlog, engineers on team)
- **Flows**: What moves between states? (users activating → churning, candidates → hires)

### Consider Second, Third, and Fourth-Order Effects
> "The skillsets that you think through and manage in a complicated ecosystem are quite different." — Hari Srinivasan

Managing complex ecosystems requires understanding effects that cascade beyond the immediate impact. Every change ripples.

**Example**: Adding a new feature (first-order) → Users adopt it (second-order) → Support burden increases (third-order) → Engineering velocity decreases (fourth-order)

### Think Beyond Today's Decisions
> "Second order thinking is you being able to think beyond the decisions that you're making today." — Nickey Skarstad

Consider how current decisions impact future constraints and ecosystem dynamics. What seems optimal today may create problems tomorrow.

### Automate Recurring Pains
> "Tell me about some process you really hated and ended up trying to automate or build a system around to make it better." — Melissa Perri + Denise Tilles

Identify recurring manual pains and build automated systems or frameworks to solve them. The best automation targets are processes people genuinely dread.

## Systems Mapping Framework

When analyzing a system, create a map with these elements:

### 1. Player Map
List all actors in the system:
- Who are they? (roles, segments, stakeholders)
- What do they want? (explicit incentives)
- What do they actually do? (observed behavior)
- How do they interact with each other?

### 2. Stock Map
What accumulates in the system:
- Users at each stage of funnel
- Features in the product
- Technical debt in codebase
- Knowledge in the organization
- Trust with customers

### 3. Flow Map
How things move between stocks:
- User acquisition → activation → retention → churn
- Feature requests → design → build → ship → adoption
- Incidents → investigation → fix → prevention

### 4. Feedback Loops
- **Reinforcing loops**: What accelerates growth? (network effects, viral loops)
- **Balancing loops**: What limits growth? (saturation, capacity constraints)

### 5. Leverage Points
Where small changes create large effects:
- Bottlenecks in flows
- High-leverage stocks
- Feedback loop interventions

## Questions to Help Users

- "Who are all the players in this system, and what does each one want?"
- "If you make this change, what happens next? And then what happens after that?"
- "What accumulates over time in this system (the stocks), and what flows between states?"
- "Where are the feedback loops—both reinforcing and balancing?"
- "What constraint, if removed, would unlock the most value in this system?"
- "What recurring manual pain could be systematized?"

## Common Mistakes to Flag

- **Only seeing first-order effects** — Changes ripple through systems in ways that aren't immediately obvious
- **Ignoring incentives** — Every player responds to their own incentives, not yours
- **Optimizing locally** — Improving one part of a system can make the whole system worse
- **Missing feedback loops** — Many systems have self-reinforcing or self-balancing dynamics that amplify or dampen changes
- **Treating symptoms instead of causes** — Systems problems often require addressing root causes, not visible symptoms
