# Anti-Patterns

Use this file to avoid common ICP and positioning mistakes.

## 1. Broad firmographic ICP

Problem:
- define ICP with vague labels like "enterprise", "mid-market", or "B2B SaaS"

Why it fails:
- too many different workflows, champions, and buying contexts hide inside those labels

Better move:
- add role, workflow, trigger, or tech-context detail

## 2. Title-only ICP

Problem:
- assume a title defines the buyer

Why it fails:
- identical titles often have very different responsibilities and urgency

Better move:
- define the job context, not just the title

## 3. Fake demand from warm channels

Problem:
- treat investor intros, friendly calls, or broad encouragement as market proof

Why it fails:
- social incentives distort the signal

Better move:
- privilege outbound and cleaner-market evidence

## 4. Pattern-match too fast from one customer

Problem:
- let one unusual customer define the whole ICP

Why it fails:
- memorable customers are often outliers

Better move:
- look for repeated attributes across multiple wins or strong opportunities

## 5. Product pessimism

Problem:
- define the market so broadly that every lost deal looks like product failure

Why it fails:
- the team stops seeing where the product already wins

Better move:
- focus on current strengths and the segment where those strengths matter most

## 6. Future-state positioning

Problem:
- position the product around what it will do later

Why it fails:
- buyers evaluate what exists now
- deals stall when the pitch outruns the product

Better move:
- position what can actually be sold today

## 7. Ignore the status quo

Problem:
- map only named competitors

Why it fails:
- many real deals are lost to "do nothing", spreadsheets, internal process, or existing tools

Better move:
- always include the status quo in the alternative set

## 8. Feature list masquerading as value

Problem:
- describe what the product does and assume buyers will connect the dots

Why it fails:
- uncommon features often require education
- buyers care about outcomes, not internal product logic

Better move:
- run the "so what?" ladder until the value lands in buyer language

## 9. Go too abstract on value

Problem:
- keep asking "so what?" until the answer becomes generic like "save time" or "improve efficiency"

Why it fails:
- the claim becomes interchangeable with every competitor

Better move:
- stop at the most specific buyer-valued outcome that still sounds meaningful

## 10. Mix value with objections

Problem:
- treat deployment ease, TCO, or change management as core value by default

Why it fails:
- those are often reasons not to buy yet, not the main reason to buy

Better move:
- separate buying motivation from buying hesitation

## 11. Treat every stakeholder equally

Problem:
- build the ICP around the entire buying committee

Why it fails:
- the work becomes vague and the main buyer logic disappears

Better move:
- anchor on the champion first, then map the rest of the stakeholders around them

## 12. Expand too early

Problem:
- broaden the segment before the initial wedge is repeatable

Why it fails:
- messaging blurs
- product focus weakens
- sales loses clarity

Better move:
- stay narrow until the win pattern is obvious and repeatable
