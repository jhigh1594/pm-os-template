# Problem Definition

Define problems clearly before jumping to solutions using frameworks from 90+ product leaders. This is the most comprehensive skill in the Lenny collection with 123 insights.

## How to Help

When the user asks for help with problem definition:

1. **Understand the current framing** — Ask how they're currently thinking about the problem
2. **Dig into the struggling moment** — Help them articulate the specific context where users feel stuck
3. **Separate problem from solution** — Ensure they haven't conflated a desired feature with the underlying need
4. **Validate the problem matters** — Help them confirm the problem is urgent and widespread enough to solve

## Core Principles

### Digitizing Analog Isn't Enough
> "Why use this instead of the Yellow Pages? It was a digital version of something that had come before." — Bret Taylor

Simply digitizing an analog predecessor often fails because it lacks a native reason to exist on the new platform. Ask "why should a customer give this the time of day?"

**Red flag**: If your value proposition is "it's [existing thing] but digital," dig deeper.

### Prototype to Define the Problem
> "This idea of getting unstuck and turning maybe some abstract ideas or some concepts that you've been discussing, turning that into a concrete prototype, something that you can look at and you can click around." — Jake Knapp + John Zeratsky

Moving from abstract concepts to concrete prototypes is the fastest way to define and solve a problem. Don't stay in discussion—build something you can react to.

**How to apply**: Before writing a PRD, create a quick prototype. The act of building reveals gaps in your problem understanding.

### Avoid the Shiny Object Trap
> "There is something called the shiny object trap, and I'm always telling people, 'Hey, don't do AI for the sake of doing AI.' Make sure there is a problem there." — Marilyn Nika

Ensure AI (or any new technology) is used to solve a specific, validated user pain point rather than for its own sake. Technology should be the answer to a question, not the question itself.

**Test**: Can you state the problem without mentioning the technology? If not, you're solution-first.

### Struggling Moments Cause Demand
> "A struggling moment causes demand. And you start to realize that in some cases that struggling moment exists and can exist for a long time and nobody solved it." — Bob Moesta

Demand is created by a specific "struggling moment" in a user's life, not by the product itself. Study the context that makes users' behavior rational. What were they trying to do when they hit the wall?

**How to apply**: Interview users about the last time they experienced the problem. What were they doing right before? What was the context?

### See the End from the Beginning
> "We are not going to start something unless we can see the end from the beginning. We're not going to take a big concept and then say, 'What's the estimate for this thing?'" — Ryan Singer

Ensure the team can visualize the completed feature before committing resources. Avoid starting with fuzzy concepts. If you can't describe what "done" looks like, you're not ready to start.

**Test**: Can you draw the finished feature? Can you describe the exact user experience?

### The Solution is What Customers Buy
> "People don't buy the problem, they buy your solution. Obviously they don't buy it if it's not solving something they care about, but there are many products that are solving what they care about." — Marty Cagan

While understanding the problem is necessary, competitive advantage comes from the quality of the solution. Don't over-rotate on problem validation if the problem is well-understood. Execution matters.

### Spend More Time on the Problem
> "Spend more time on the problem than the solution." — Christopher Lochhead

Deeply understanding the customer's perspective of the problem is more valuable than internal product brainstorming. Listen to hear their perspective rather than just pitching your solution.

### Qualify the Problem Type
> "I don't know that we even talk about problems without a qualifier. Are we talking about a business problem? Are we talking about a customer problem?" — Christopher Miller

Effective problem definition requires distinguishing between business needs and customer pain points to avoid "customer-hostile" solutions. Not all business problems are customer problems.

## Problem Definition Framework

### 1. The Struggling Moment
- What is the user doing right before they encounter this problem?
- What were they trying to accomplish?
- What context were they in? (rushed, distracted, mobile, etc.)
- How were they feeling?

### 2. Current Workarounds
- What are people doing today to work around this problem?
- How painful is the workaround? (If it's not painful, there's no demand)
- What would make them switch from workaround to your solution?

### 3. Problem Qualification
- Is this a **business problem** or a **customer problem**?
- Are they aligned or in conflict?
- How urgent is this? How frequent?

### 4. Solution Boundary
- What would solve this problem? (without specifying HOW)
- What's the minimum that would help?
- What's the ideal experience?

### 5. Validation Signals
- How would we know if we've defined the problem correctly?
- What data could validate this?
- What would invalidate our assumptions?

## Questions to Help Users

- "What is the user doing right before they encounter this problem? What are they trying to accomplish?"
- "Why hasn't this problem been solved already? What makes it hard?"
- "Is this a business problem or a customer problem? Are they the same?"
- "If you solved this problem perfectly, how would the user's life be different?"
- "Can you describe a specific person experiencing this problem in a specific moment?"
- "What are people doing today to work around this problem?"

## Common Mistakes to Flag

- **Solution-first thinking** — Starting with a feature idea and working backward to justify it
- **Technology-first thinking** — Wanting to use AI/blockchain/etc. and looking for problems to apply it to
- **Abstract problem statements** — Defining problems so broadly they don't point to any specific solution
- **Conflating business and customer problems** — Solving for internal metrics without addressing user needs
- **Skipping the struggling moment** — Not understanding the specific context where pain occurs

## Problem Statement Template

```markdown
# Problem: [Title]

## The Struggling Moment
[Specific context where user experiences pain]

**User was trying to**: [What they wanted to accomplish]
**Context**: [Situation - rushed, mobile, first time, etc.]
**Feeling**: [Frustrated, confused, anxious, etc.]

## Current Workarounds
- [What they do today]
- [How painful is it: 1-10]

## Problem Type
- [ ] Business problem: [what business needs]
- [ ] Customer problem: [what user needs]
- [ ] Aligned / In conflict: [explain]

## Success Criteria
If we've solved this problem, the user will be able to:
1. [Specific outcome 1]
2. [Specific outcome 2]

## Validation Signals
- [What data would confirm this problem exists]
- [What would prove us wrong]
```
